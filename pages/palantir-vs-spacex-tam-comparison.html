Palantir vs. SpaceX: Total Addressable Market (TAM) Comparison

Not affiliated with Palantir Technologies Inc. This page explains what each company claims about its market opportunity, and how skeptical outside analysis has responded — it does not independently verify either figure. Nothing here is financial advice.

Both Palantir and SpaceX make large, ambitious claims about the size of the market they're chasing — and both sets of claims have drawn real skepticism from outside analysts, which is worth understanding alongside the headline numbers.

Palantir's stated market opportunity

At its 2020 direct listing, Palantir estimated its total addressable market at roughly $119 billion, split between a $56 billion commercial opportunity and a $63 billion government opportunity. That figure predates the 2023 launch of AIP, Palantir's AI platform, and more recent analyst commentary has framed the opportunity in far larger terms — Wedbush analyst Dan Ives has described a global AI market opportunity approaching $1 trillion that Palantir is positioned to capture a share of, and some bullish 2026 commentary has floated AIP's own "strategic value" at $500 billion or more, alongside speculation about Palantir eventually reaching trillion-dollar market capitalization. These larger figures are analyst and commentator estimates, not numbers Palantir itself has formally re-published as an updated official TAM.

SpaceX's stated market opportunity

SpaceX's claims are considerably larger and more specific. Around its 2026 IPO, the company reportedly cited a total addressable market of $28.5 trillion, broken into three components: $22.7 trillion for "enterprise applications," $4.3 trillion for consumer/telecom services, and $1.5 trillion for the core space economy (launch, satellites, and related infrastructure).

The skepticism, on both sides

SpaceX's $28.5 trillion figure in particular has drawn direct, pointed criticism. Analysts have questioned how a rocket and satellite company arrives at $22.7 trillion in "enterprise applications" TAM — a category that sounds more like software and cloud computing than aerospace — with some commentary describing the framing as an attempt to justify a lofty valuation with a market-sizing exercise that doesn't obviously connect to SpaceX's actual current business lines. Palantir has faced a related but different critique: its stock trades at a valuation multiple many analysts describe as pricing in years of TAM capture that hasn't happened yet, discussed further on our page about why Palantir's stock is so volatile.

Why TAM figures deserve extra skepticism generally

Total addressable market estimates are inherently speculative — they represent a company's or analyst's judgment about how large a market could become, not a measured, audited figure the way revenue is. Companies have a structural incentive to claim large TAMs, since a bigger claimed opportunity can support a higher valuation multiple regardless of near-term revenue. Both Palantir's and SpaceX's TAM claims should be read with that incentive in mind, rather than treated as neutral, third-party market research.

The core difference in framing

Even setting aside the specific dollar figures, the two companies are making different kinds of claims. Palantir's TAM claims are grounded primarily in software and AI-platform categories — markets that already exist and are already being measured by independent research firms. SpaceX's largest claimed category, "enterprise applications," represents a more speculative bet that space-based infrastructure (satellite connectivity, in-space computing, and similar) will eventually capture meaningful share of markets that today are served entirely by terrestrial technology — a genuinely different, higher-uncertainty kind of claim than Palantir's.
