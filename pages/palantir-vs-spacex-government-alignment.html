Palantir vs. SpaceX: Government Alignment Compared

Not affiliated with Palantir Technologies Inc. This page is an independent explainer based on publicly available sources. See our dedicated page on Palantir's government contracts for the fuller Palantir-specific list.

Both Palantir and SpaceX depend heavily on U.S. government relationships, but the shape of that dependency — which agencies, how concentrated, and how politically exposed — differs meaningfully between the two.

Palantir's government relationships

Palantir's government revenue is spread across defense, intelligence, and civilian agencies: the U.S. Army and Navy, the intelligence community, ICE, the IRS, the Social Security Administration, and allied governments including the UK's Ministry of Defence and NHS — all detailed on our consolidated government contracts page. As of mid-2025, government revenue represented roughly 55% of Palantir's total revenue, meaning the company remains majority-dependent on public-sector spending even as its commercial business grows faster in percentage terms.

SpaceX's government relationships

SpaceX's government ties run through NASA and the Department of Defense, and are arguably even more foundational to the company's history than Palantir's are to its own. NASA's Commercial Crew and Commercial Resupply programs were critical early revenue and validation for SpaceX's Falcon 9 and Dragon spacecraft; more recently, SpaceX has taken on high-profile national security launch contracts and is a central contractor for the U.S. Space Force's launch program. SpaceX has also become deeply embedded in Pentagon satellite communications and reconnaissance efforts through its classified Starshield programs.

A key structural difference: sole-source dependency

One of the sharpest differences is how replaceable each company is to its government customers. In launch specifically, SpaceX has achieved a level of dominance that gives the U.S. government limited near-term alternatives for certain missions — a position of leverage, but also one that draws its own scrutiny over concentration risk if a single company controls that much of a critical capability. Palantir, by contrast, operates in a more genuinely multi-vendor environment: competitors like Anduril, traditional contractors like Booz Allen Hamilton and Deloitte, and other AI/data platforms all compete for government data-analytics work, discussed on our page about Palantir's competitors — meaning agencies generally have more realistic alternatives to Palantir than to SpaceX for orbital launch specifically.

Political exposure differs in kind

Palantir's government work has drawn sustained political controversy tied to the nature of what its software does — enabling ICE enforcement actions, IRS data-mining, and domestic-surveillance-adjacent capabilities, covered throughout our Controversies section. SpaceX's government exposure is more about concentration and personality risk: CEO Elon Musk's public political involvement and his high-profile position in the 2025 Department of Government Efficiency initiative (which also touched Palantir, detailed on our DOGE page) created a distinct kind of reputational entanglement between SpaceX's government contracts and its CEO's personal political activity — a risk profile Palantir's own leadership, while also politically outspoken at times, hasn't generated in quite the same form.

Why this matters for each company's risk profile

Government dependency cuts both ways for both companies: it provides large, relatively stable revenue streams, but it also means both companies' fortunes are partially tied to political dynamics neither fully controls — a change in administration priorities, a public controversy, or a shift in procurement strategy can move the needle on both stocks in ways that have nothing to do with product execution.
